# v13.45 — Review of the site audit and the ten-innovation brief

Architect assessment, 2026-08-15, of two further external strategy documents submitted by the
Founder: a 360° site audit scoring alparai.com, and a ten-point innovation brief for the 2026
ecosystem.

**First, credit where it is due.** The innovation brief opens by adopting v13.44's issuer-pays
finding as a hard rule. That is a real improvement over the previous brief and the reasoning
carried correctly. The problem is that the rule is stated as a slogan rather than a test, so it
is reintroduced three times later in the same document without anyone noticing.

## The payer test — proposed doctrine

A ban on "issuer-pays" is unenforceable because every proposal renames the payment. What is
enforceable is a question:

> **Does the party paying benefit from a favourable result?**

If yes, it is issuer-pays regardless of the label — audit fee, compliance badge, scanning
licence, red-team campaign. If no, it is clean. Applying it to the brief's own proposals:

| Proposal                            | Payer                      | Benefits from a good result? | Verdict         |
| ----------------------------------- | -------------------------- | ---------------------------- | --------------- |
| Training-data provenance audit $10K | the company being audited  | yes                          | **issuer-pays** |
| Compliance-as-Evidence badge $5K/yr | the company under scrutiny | yes, directly reduces a fine | **issuer-pays** |
| Red-team campaign $50K              | the company being tested   | yes                          | **issuer-pays** |
| Academic licence                    | university                 | no                           | clean           |
| Media/journalist access             | newsroom                   | no                           | clean           |
| Grant funding                       | foundation                 | no                           | clean           |
| Individual certification            | the person certified       | no — they are not audited    | clean           |

Compliance-as-Evidence is the sharpest case and should be rejected outright: it sells mitigation
to a party under regulatory investigation, with ALPAR vouching for good faith to the regulator.
That is not a business model, it is an exposure.

## Fabricated precision, again

The site audit reports "859/1000", awards "+16 points" for a case-study page and "+24" for an
about page, and projects "859 → 945 in 72 hours". No such scale exists; the numbers are
invented and the deltas are unfalsifiable. The observations underneath several of them are real
and worth acting on — but the scoring apparatus should not enter the record or a deck, for the
same reason the ratings page now labels its own unmeasured categories.

The same document cites "992 cases" in its closing while its page-level analysis says 371. It
contradicts itself on the corpus size, which is precisely what STRAT-1 exists to settle.

## Two claims in the site audit are factually wrong

**"TR=13 vs EN=371 incident count — data integrity violation."** The count query in
`src/app/[locale]/page.tsx:75` (`getHomeData`) applies **no locale filter**: it is
`.from("incidents").select("id", { count: "exact", head: true }).eq("status", "published")`,
identical for every locale. This code path cannot produce a per-language difference. If two
different numbers were genuinely observed, the likely cause is two page loads separated by one
of the day's twenty deployments, not a data-integrity defect. Re-observe with timestamps before
treating it as a bug.

**"Leaderboard is missing a response-rate column — the biggest missing lever."** `response_rate`
and `response_count` already exist on `ai_providers` and are already selected by the same
`getHomeData` query. The data is present and unrendered. This is an afternoon of UI work, not a
data-model change — and the underlying product insight is correct and worth doing.

## What to take

**Agent Action Registry — the strongest idea in either document.** Autonomous agents now take
consequential actions (purchases, contracts, outbound email) with no durable record, there is no
incumbent, and it extends the existing thesis rather than pivoting from it. Critically, it is a
**schema extension to `incidents`**, not a new product: an action type, an authority field
(was it authorised), and an impact field. Build it as fields, not as a platform.

The brief's revenue model for it is fantasy — regulators do not purchase data from startups —
but that does not damage the product idea. It is funded the same way the rest is: not at all,
initially, which is correct for an evidence asset.

**Response-rate display on the leaderboard.** The data exists. It is also the one metric a
provider can improve, which turns the leaderboard from purely punitive into something that
rewards engagement — without any payment changing hands.

**DMCA and content-moderation policies.** A genuine legal gap. A platform hosting third-party
allegations about named companies needs a published notice-and-action procedure; under the EU
DSA this is the difference between having a safe harbour and not. Cheap and overdue.

**A filing helper, narrowly scoped.** The Founder has just filed with seven institutions by
hand and holds real knowledge of the formats. The useful product is helping **one** user file
correctly in **their own** jurisdiction, with templates and a status tracker. The brief's
version — automatic submission to fifty regulators — is the "diplomatic cable" idea from the
previous brief in new clothing, and carries the same defamation and jurisdiction problems.
Regulators will block a channel that floods them with out-of-jurisdiction filings.

## What to reject, with reasons

**AI Incident Futures Market.** A betting market on whether companies will cause harm creates a
financial incentive for participants to want incidents to happen, and for a minority to cause
them. For an accountability body this is disqualifying on its own; gambling regulation in most
jurisdictions is the second problem.

**Shadow AI Discovery.** Network-traffic scanning inside customer environments is a different
company — endpoint agents, SOC 2, an enterprise security sales motion — competing with
established vendors. Not adjacent; a pivot.

**AI Incident Insurance Pool.** Paying claims makes ALPAR a regulated insurer or managing
general agent. That is a licensing project, not a feature.

**Deepfake / provenance verification API.** ALPAR told a national outlet this week that a
detector with an unpublished error rate cannot be used as evidence. Selling an API that outputs
"AI-generated probability: 94%" without publishing its own false-positive rate refutes that
argument with its own product. There is a version that works — publish the error rate, and the
honesty becomes the differentiator — but the brief does not propose it.

**Whistleblower portal, partially.** `src/actions/whistleblower.ts` already exists, so the
capability is not new. Running a Tor hidden service for AI-lab insiders is a serious operational
and legal commitment that makes the organisation a target, and should not be undertaken
casually. The foundation-grant angle (Ford, MacArthur, Knight) is genuinely worth pursuing and
is independent of the Tor question. The claim that a five-model panel can "verify" an insider
report about unpublished internal test results is not credible — no model can verify a fact it
has no access to.

## Recommendation

Take the Agent Action Registry as a schema extension, ship the response-rate column, publish the
two legal policies, and scope the filing helper to one user in one jurisdiction. Reject the rest
of the P0/P1 list. Adopt the payer test as doctrine so the next brief can be checked against a
rule rather than a preference.
